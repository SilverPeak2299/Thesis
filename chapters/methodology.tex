\chapter{Methodology}
\noindent
This chapter outlines the methodology used to design, construct, and evaluate a
DevSecOps toolchain that governs ETL workflows in regulated environments. The
research applies a Design Science Research (DSR) approach, chosen for its focus
on building artifacts that address real-world problems while generating
transferable knowledge \cite{hevner_design_2004}. Rather than developing a new
ETL pipeline, this study designs the system that governs such pipelines—the
architecture, control automation, and controls that embed assurance and
compliance into operational data workflows.

\section{Design Science Context}
\noindent
Design Science generates knowledge through the purposeful creation and
evaluation of artifacts \cite{peffers_design_2007}. It follows an iterative
build–evaluate process in which understanding arises from constructing,
implementing, and refining solutions. In this study, the artifact is a
\textit{DevSecOps governance and assurance framework}—a set of integrated
components that enforce policy, record lineage and evidence metadata, and assure
integrity across an ETL pipeline. The pipeline itself functions as a
\textit{reference pipeline} that represents a typical control-baseline
environment within a regulated domain.

This methodological choice is appropriate because Design Science balances
\textit{rigour}—alignment with regulatory and theoretical principles—with
\textit{relevance}—demonstrated utility in practice. Each design iteration:
problem identification, artifact construction, demonstration, and evaluation,
corresponds to a development stage where the framework’s assurance features are
incrementally refined and tested. This cyclical process mirrors the continuous
control monitoring model advocated in DevSecOps, where systems evolve through
assurance evidence and feedback.

\section{Regulatory Context and Requirement Derivation}
\noindent
The toolchain design is guided by the Australian Prudential Regulation
Authority’s (APRA) Prudential Standards: CPS~230
(\textit{Operational~Risk~Management}), CPS~234
(\textit{Information~Security}), and CPS~510 (\textit{Governance})
\cite{cps230,cps234,cps510}. These standards define what regulated entities
must achieve in governance, security, and resilience, but they do not prescribe
how those objectives should be implemented technically. Their descriptive,
outcomes-based language demands interpretation into enforceable engineering
controls.

Although the methodology is domain-agnostic, this research focuses on the
\textit{financial sector}. Finance offers a mature and well-documented
regulatory landscape that provides both conceptual depth and practical
relevance for governance evaluation. APRA’s prudential standards form a
coherent, enforceable set of requirements that align with the objectives of
assurance, lineage, and accountability identified in the literature. This focus
reflects the local regulatory environment and the availability of structured
compliance expectations that make the sector suitable for Design Science
evaluation.

Accordingly, the scope is narrowed to a practical subset of CPS requirements
most relevant to data-pipeline governance. The selected principles—continuity,
accountability, and control—are translated into implementable requirements for
control automation and evidence generation. This refinement is informed by the
literature review in Chapter~2, which frames auditability (as defined there) in
terms of lineage, evidence, and verification across socio-technical systems.

\section{Requirements}
\noindent
The following requirements define what the DevSecOps framework must achieve to
operationalise prudential expectations. They establish the conditions under
which the framework can govern a pipeline by embedding control, lineage, and
continuous assurance.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{R1: Governance Accountability (CPS~510).}  
        The framework must preserve authorship and approval lineage for all
        operational and configuration changes, ensuring full traceability of
        decision authority and execution.

  \item \textbf{R2: Information Security Assurance (CPS~234).}  
        The framework must maintain artifact integrity and confidentiality
        through tamper-evident storage, cryptographic verification, and
        continuous security scanning (SCA, SAST, and DAST).

  \item \textbf{R3: Operational Risk Control (CPS~230).}  
        The framework must detect, record, and remediate process or
        configuration failures, maintaining reproducibility and recovery within
        defined assurance thresholds.

  \item \textbf{R4: Continuous Control Monitoring (CPS~234,~510).}  
        The framework must automate policy validation and control testing in the
        CI/CD process, producing machine-verifiable assurance evidence through
        policy-as-code enforcement.

  \item \textbf{R5: End-to-End Evidence Chain (CPS~230).}  
        The framework must generate and retain a tamper-evident, queryable
        record that captures the lineage of code, configuration, and data
        artifacts throughout the system lifecycle.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
These requirements shift focus from the business logic of ETL processes to the
\textit{meta-layer} of assurance—how evidence is produced, tested, and
preserved. They align with the five auditability dimensions defined in
Section~2.2: normativity, accountability, referentiality, reliability, and
verifiability.

\noindent
Governance and control monitoring (R1,~R4) express the normative and accountable
aspects of control; security and risk management (R2,~R3) reinforce reliability
and traceability; and the end-to-end evidence chain (R5) provides verifiable,
referential links across artifacts and executions. Together, these create a
governance and assurance framework where assurance is continuous and evidence is
intrinsic.

\section{Evaluation Framework}
\noindent
Evaluation in Design Science verifies that an artifact fulfils its intended
purpose. Here, the goal is to confirm that the DevSecOps framework meets each
requirement (R1–R5). Each requirement becomes an evaluation unit with defined
control indicators, data sources, and assurance thresholds.

\subsection*{R1 Governance Accountability}
\noindent
\textbf{Objective:} Confirm that all changes are traceable to authenticated
identities and documented approvals. 

\textbf{Method:} Examine change events, approval records, and version metadata
for completeness and signature validity.  

\textbf{Control Indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item 100\% of configuration and code changes linked to a verified identity.
  \item 100\% of recorded approvals associated with tamper-evident artifacts.
  \item Continuous lineage maintained across system versions.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Auditability Dimensions:} Normativity, Accountability.

\subsection*{R2 Information Security Assurance}
\noindent
\textbf{Objective:} Demonstrate that artifacts and configurations are verified
for integrity and security.  

\textbf{Method:} Conduct automated and manual reviews confirming that artifacts
are signed, validated, and free from known vulnerabilities.  

\textbf{Control Indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item 100\% of artifacts validated through checksum or signature verification.
  \item Zero undetected tampering events during testing.
  \item Complete retention of verification records.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Auditability Dimensions:} Reliability, Verifiability.

\subsection*{R3 Operational Risk Control}
\noindent
\textbf{Objective:} Validate reproducibility and recovery from prior workflow
states.  

\textbf{Method:} Execute controlled replays of historical states and compare
outputs to reference baselines.  

\textbf{Control Indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\geq$95\% lineage completeness between input, process, and output.
  \item Successful re-execution of three or more historical states.
  \item Recovery times within defined assurance thresholds.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Auditability Dimensions:} Referentiality, Reliability.

\subsection*{R4 Continuous Control Monitoring}
\noindent
\textbf{Objective:} Verify that compliance enforcement and validation operate
automatically for every change.  

\textbf{Method:} Observe multiple workflow executions to confirm policy checks
and validations occur consistently.  

\textbf{Control Indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\geq$95\% of executions meet all control criteria.
  \item No unapproved or unverified changes reach production.
  \item All control evaluations generate verifiable records.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Auditability Dimensions:} Normativity, Accountability.

\subsection*{R5 End-to-End Evidence Chain}
\noindent
\textbf{Objective:} Confirm that the framework maintains a tamper-evident,
queryable record of artifacts and lineage.  

\textbf{Method:} Perform end-to-end tracing of artifacts across executions to
ensure each output can be reconstructed from recorded evidence.  

\textbf{Control Indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item 100\% of evidence and lineage records validate without error.
  \item All transformations traceable to original inputs.
  \item Zero discrepancies in signature or hash verification tests.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Auditability Dimensions:} Referentiality, Verifiability.

\subsection*{Evaluation Synthesis}
\noindent
Each requirement is tested through direct observation and measurement, with
results mapped back to the auditability dimensions in
Table~\ref{tab:req-auditability}. Collectively, these evaluations determine
whether the governance and assurance framework establishes a verifiable
end-to-end evidence chain across its lifecycle. The analysis confirms not only
functional correctness but also the degree to which the artifact embodies the
principles of continuous assurance defined in Chapter~2.
